12 cyclists hit

At Elgin Street and Laurier Avenue, twelve people on bikes were struck by drivers between 2015 and 2019. Five in 2016 alone, the worst single year at any location in the city.

Elgin Street here is a route the City of Ottawa recommends cyclists take. It is a “suggested route”: no barrier, no separation. Just paint and a sign.

Zoom out

Ottawa's “suggested” network

Elgin isn't unusual. The city has drawn roughly 487 km of suggested routes: ordinary roads it marks as cycling routes without building anything to protect the people riding them.

Three tiers of “bike infrastructure”

The network is a hierarchy. Protected lanes physically separate bikes from traffic. Painted lanes mark a space with a stripe. And suggested routes, by far the most common, offer neither.

Only about 39 km of the network is physically protected. Suggested routes outnumber it more than twelve to one.

Now add the crashes

Every red dot is a location where a cyclist was struck between 2015 and 2019: 1,416 cyclist collisions in all. Bigger dots mean more crashes at that spot.

Watch where they land.

They cluster on the routes the city recommends

451 of those collisions, across 314 locations, happened on the suggested-route network itself, the streets the city tells cyclists to use.

At 75 of those locations, cyclists were hit more than once over the five years. Repeatedly, on the same unprotected streets.

The bigger picture

This isn't one bad corner

Elgin & Laurier is the worst, but it sits at the top of a long list. These are the suggested-route locations where Ottawa cyclists were struck most often over five years.

Every bar is a recommended route

None of these locations offered physical protection. Each one is a place the city's own network marks as a route to ride, and each one hit multiple cyclists.

Read these numbers carefully

We're counting collisions, not comparing crash rates between lane types. Protected lanes are built exactly where cycling is busiest, so a naïve “crashes per kilometre” comparison is misleading, and we don't have ridership data to correct for it.

What the data does show plainly: real, repeated harm is accumulating on the unprotected routes Ottawa actively recommends.

What “upgrading” looks like

Paint is not infrastructure.

Turning a suggested route into a protected one means a curb, a bollard, a raised separation: something physical between a person on a bike and two tonnes of moving metal. Ottawa has done it before: Laurier Avenue's own segregated lane, a block from the worst intersection in this story, is proof it's possible.

Bike Ottawa has mapped this gap for years and pushes the City to close it. The City's cycling infrastructure plans decide which suggested routes finally get built for real.

Suggested isn't safe. It's just cheaper.

Methodology & sources

Data

Two City of Ottawa open datasets: the Ottawa Cycling Network (route geometry classified by type, including “Suggested Route”) and Traffic Collisions by Location, 2015–2019 (collision counts by location, with per-year cyclist and pedestrian breakdowns).

Time window

All collision figures cover 2015–2019, a fixed five-year historical window, not current-year data. Ottawa's network has changed since; some suggested routes may have been upgraded or re-classified.

“On a suggested route”

A collision is counted as on the suggested network if it falls within 5 metres of a “Suggested Route” line: the same spatial test used in the project's original prototype. Field naming in the source is inconsistent (e.g. F2015_Cyclist vs F2016_Cyclists); both forms are handled.

Limitations

Collision points are geocoded to locations, not exact impact coordinates, so the 5 m test is approximate near intersections. The worst location, Elgin & Laurier, is where a suggested route crosses a protected corridor. We report collision counts, not exposure-adjusted rates: without ridership volumes, per-kilometre comparisons between lane types would be misleading and are deliberately avoided.
